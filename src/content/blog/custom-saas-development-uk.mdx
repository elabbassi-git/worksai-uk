## What "custom SaaS development" actually means

SaaS stands for software as a service: software your customers use in a browser, usually by signing in to their own account. **Custom** SaaS development means building that platform specifically for your product or business, instead of configuring a general tool.

In practice, a production SaaS platform is more than the feature your customers pay for. Underneath it sits a layer of plumbing that every serious product needs:

- **Accounts and authentication:** sign-up, log-in, password resets, two-step verification
- **Organisations and roles:** a customer can invite colleagues, each with the right permissions
- **Multi-tenancy:** every customer's data is kept separate from everyone else's
- **Payments:** checkout, invoices and receipts, usually through a provider such as Stripe
- **Admin:** a back office for your team to support customers and see what is happening
- **Email, notifications and audit logs:** so users know what happened, and you can prove it

A prototype skips most of this. A platform you can sell cannot.

<Callout type="key" title="Platform first, features second">
  Most failed SaaS builds don't fail on the clever feature. They fail on the plumbing: permissions leaking between customers, payments that don't reconcile, or a database nobody can change safely. Get the foundations right and features become cheap to add.
</Callout>

## When custom SaaS makes sense (and when it doesn't)

Building your own platform is a serious investment. It is the right call when:

- you are launching a **software product** that customers will pay to use
- your business runs on a process **no off-the-shelf tool fits** without painful workarounds
- you are paying for **several tools per user** and still copying data between them
- you need to give **your own customers** a branded portal to log in, track work and pay

It is usually the wrong call when an existing tool already does 90% of what you need. Then a good set-up, or a small integration, will save you time and money. A sensible studio will tell you that before quoting.

### 1. Discovery: define the first release

The single most valuable thing you can do is make the first release **small and sharp**. Write down who your first users are, the one job they need done, and what "done" looks like. Everything else goes on a later list. A clear scope is also what makes a fixed price possible.

### 2. Architecture: decide the foundations

This is where the data model, technology stack and security approach are agreed. For most UK SaaS products, proven choices are the right ones: TypeScript, React, Node.js and PostgreSQL, with Stripe for payments and Cloudflare or AWS for hosting. Boring technology is a feature. It means any good developer can maintain your product after launch.

### 3. Design: make it real before building it

A clickable design of every key screen catches misunderstandings while they are cheap to fix. It is also the best way to test the product with a few real users before a line of code is written.

### 4. Build in short sprints

Two-week sprints, each ending with a demo on a staging site, keep everyone honest. You see real progress, give feedback early, and can re-prioritise if you learn something new.

### 5. Harden and launch

Before launch, the platform needs automated tests, performance checks and a security review covering access control, input validation, rate limiting, backups and error monitoring. Then it is deployed to infrastructure in **your** name.

## Multi-tenancy and security: the parts you can't see

If your platform serves more than one customer organisation, tenant isolation is non-negotiable. A common, robust approach is to tag every record with an organisation and enforce access in the database itself, for example with PostgreSQL row-level security, so that a bug in one screen cannot expose another customer's data.

You also need to think about UK GDPR from the start: what personal data you collect, why, where it is stored, who can access it and how long you keep it. Building those rules into the platform is far easier than retrofitting them.

## Owning your platform: avoiding lock-in

Ask any studio three questions before you sign:

1. **Whose name are the code repository and cloud accounts in?** They should be yours from day one.
2. **What documentation do I get at hand-over?** Enough for another developer to continue.
3. **What do I pay after launch?** Ideally, only your own hosting and third-party services, paid directly to those providers.

<Callout type="warning" title="Beware the hidden retainer">
  Some agencies build in their own accounts and charge a monthly fee to keep your product running. That can look cheap at the start and become very expensive to leave. Make ownership part of the contract.
</Callout>
