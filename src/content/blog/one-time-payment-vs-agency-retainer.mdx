## Two ways agencies charge

When you hire an agency to build a website, an AI assistant or a SaaS platform, you will usually see one of two models:

- **One-time payment:** a fixed price for a clearly defined piece of work. When it is delivered, you own it and the transaction is complete.
- **Retainer:** a monthly fee for ongoing work, support or hosting, often with a minimum term. Some agencies combine a lower build price with a mandatory retainer.

Neither is wrong in itself. The question is which model matches the work you actually need.

## Think in total cost, not the first invoice

## Ownership: the question that decides everything

The biggest difference between models is often not price but **control**. If the agency builds in its own hosting account, owns your domain or keeps the code in its own repository, a retainer is not really optional: you are paying rent on your own system.

<Callout type="warning" title="Signs of lock-in">
  Your domain is registered in the agency's name. You don't have admin access to your hosting or cloud account. The code isn't in a repository you control. There is no hand-over documentation. Any of these makes leaving slow and expensive.
</Callout>

## When a retainer genuinely makes sense

Retainers can be the right choice when the work really is continuous: for example a large platform with a dedicated team shipping new features every week, or ongoing paid advertising management. In those cases you are buying a team's time, and a retainer is an honest way to pay for it.

What you should avoid is a retainer that exists only because the agency kept control of things you paid to have built.

## The verdict

For builds and launches, a one-time payment gives you cost certainty, ownership and freedom to choose who works on your system next. Retainers have their place for genuinely continuous work, but never as a condition of owning what you paid for.
